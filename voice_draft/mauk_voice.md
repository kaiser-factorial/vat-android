Maurits Cornelis Escher was born into an aristocratic, highly educated family (his father was a civil engineer), which heavily dictates how he would have articulated his words.

If you were to step back in time to his studio in Baarn, here is a breakdown of how his voice likely sounded:

1. The Dutch "Polder" Sophistication
Escher likely spoke with a "Haags" or highly refined Dutch accent. In his era, the upper class spoke with a very distinct, polished clarity.

The "R": Unlike the rolling "R" heard in many parts of the Netherlands, an aristocrat like Escher might have used a slight uvular "R" (similar to a soft French "R"), which was a sign of status.

The "G": His "G" would have been "soft"—a velvety, breathy sound from the back of the throat—rather than the harsh, scraping "G" (the "hard G") associated with Amsterdam or the Northern provinces.

2. His English Pronunciation
Had he spoken to you in English, it wouldn't have sounded like the modern, Americanized English many Dutch people speak today. It would have been "Continental English":

Precise and Clipped: He would have pronounced every consonant with mathematical precision. The Dutch language requires active mouth movement, so his English would have lacked the "mumble" of a native speaker.

Vowel Shifts: He likely would have struggled with the "th" sound (turning "the" into "de" or "ze") and the "v/f" distinction. However, given his intellect, these would have been very subtle.

The Lilt: Dutch has a specific "up-and-down" cadence. Even in English, he probably would have had a rhythmic, melodic way of ending his sentences.
